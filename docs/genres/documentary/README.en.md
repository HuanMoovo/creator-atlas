## 1. The Method & Applicable Types

The essence of real-life documentary: turning "uncontrollable reality" into watchable content. Unlike scripted methods, events exist before the script; the creator's work is selecting, following and reassembling, not choreographing. Two core abilities follow: **judging what is worth recording**, and **keeping structure amid chaos**.

Authenticity has a premium and a price: viewers tolerate more roughness but demand less "designed-ness." Staging that gets seen through destroys the whole piece instantly.

### Applicable Types in Detail

| Type | Characteristics | Typical Length | Production Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Daily vlog | Personality continuity and companionship | 5–15 min | Soften information, strengthen personality; rhythm from music and time jumps |
| Travel documentary | Two routes: imagery value or information value | 8–25 min | Guide-style builds on information beats; cinematic-style builds on shots and mood |
| Food & cooking | Process recording and appetite, converted audio-visually | 3–12 min | Sound and close-ups are the core asset; shoot the finished dish while it is perfect |
| Food tours & shop visits | Balancing information efficiency and honesty | 3–10 min | Cover price, portions and location; keep disclosure boundaries clear |
| Pets & parenting | Relationship narratives and patience | 3–10 min | Candid capture over long accumulation; mind on-camera and privacy boundaries |
| Outdoor & sports | Risk narratives and safety expression | 8–20 min | Clear goals, visible risks; safety and permits outrank shots |
| Rural & farming | Process cycles and seasonal narratives | 5–15 min | Long-term filming; cycle changes are the natural structure |
| Building & renovation | Recording visible progress | 5–15 min | Fixed-camera progress shots; milestones become chapters |
| Weddings & events | Delivery perspective: host goals and guest memories | 3–10 min (finished cut) | Two cuts: broadcast version and keepsake version |
| ASMR & ambience | Sensory experience, weak narrative | 5–60 min | Sound quality is the content; strip the environment down |
| Home & lifestyle | Setting aesthetics and practical information | 5–12 min | Control light and ambient noise; tidy the frame before shooting |
| Street observation | Chance and city narratives | 3–10 min | On-the-spot judgment; portrait and privacy handling must be compliant |
| Fishing & outdoors | Slow process, then the payoff moment | 5–20 min | Compress the waiting, enlarge the catch; ambient sound sets the mood |
| Cycling & road trips | Route process and roadside finds | 8–20 min | Route info and imagery together; safety and weather backup |

### Boundaries & Scope

- "Pseudo-documentary" with clear planning and scripting, where staging outweighs real recording, is treated as Scripted & Performance.
- Wedding and event records delivered to clients follow the Commercial Production workflow; personal-perspective records stay in this method.
- When documentary and talking-head mix (walking and explaining), what decides is whether the footage comes from a real scene: if yes, it belongs here.

## 2. Key Principles

1. **Footage before structure.** Documentary follows "collect while shooting, discover while editing"; a fully written script wastes the scene, yet shooting with no goal collects nothing usable.
2. **An event needs a promise.** Daily life is raw material, not content. Every piece must still answer "why watch to the end": a goal, a suspense, or a change.
3. **Authenticity comes from checkable detail.** Times, places, costs, mistakes — concrete numbers and flaws build trust better than polish; a flawless "daily life" looks fake instead.
4. **Companionship comes from a continuous personality.** A steady viewpoint, tone and value set (what excites you, what you pick on) forms subscription expectations; this is the highest-yield asset in lifestyle content.
5. **Documentary has three acts too.** Setup (goal and circumstance) → development (difficulties and change) → landing (result and feeling). Without a landing, the piece feels unfinished.
6. **Editing is authorship.** What to keep, what to cut, how to leap across time — the decisions themselves are style; the line between a log and a work is drawn at the editing table.
7. **Deliver at least one of information or emotion.** Travel and shop visits deliver practical information; vlogs and pets deliver companionship. With neither, only a diary remains.

## 3. Standard Workflow

Documentary works the opposite way from scripted content: the script only takes final shape on the editing table. The front end supplies **a goal and a shooting plan**, the back end does **discovery and reconstruction**. The quality of footage management therefore sets the ceiling on the finished piece.

| Phase | Key Actions | Output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Planning & Promise | Pick one of goal / suspense / change; survey scene and budget | Shooting goal card |
| 2. Shooting Plan | Shot list, permits and safety, itinerary and backups | Shot list and contingency plan |
| 3. Field Capture | Follow-shooting, pickups, ambient sound, field notes | Field footage and shooting log |
| 4. Footage Organization | Transcode or proxy, tag, discard decisions | Searchable footage library |
| 5. Structural Rough Cut | Paper edit: setup → development → landing | Structural cut |
| 6. Fine Cut | Rhythm, music, captions, light color treatment | Master file |
| 7. Publish & Review | Titles and thumbnails, publish settings, data review | Publish pack and review notes |

### Phase 1: Planning & Promise

- Pick one of "a goal, a suspense, a change" as the reason to keep watching; if you cannot write it down, do not start shooting.
- Survey the scene: light conditions, sound environment, permits, safety risks — one conclusion each.
- Output: the shooting goal card. Checkpoint: you can answer "why watch to the end" in one sentence.

### Phase 2: Shooting Plan

- Build the shot list: must-get shots (setup / process / landing), pickup types, total time budget.
- Handle permits for locations, people and equipment in advance; write the contingency plan for each risk item.
- Output: shot list and contingency plan. Checkpoint: every must-get shot has a shooting window arranged.

### Phase 3: Field Capture

- Follow-shoot to keep the process; shoot pickups to keep editing freedom: environment, details, reactions all need margin.
- Log time, place, people and key points for later context; record ambient sound separately.
- Output: field footage and shooting log. Checkpoint: every key moment has usable material, and footage runs at least 5× the finished piece.

### Phase 4: Footage Organization

- Transcode (or generate proxies), back up and tag the same day; discard obvious rejects.
- Tag into "usable setup / usable development / usable landing" rather than piles by timecode.
- Output: a searchable footage library. Checkpoint: an editor can find each key type without reading the log.

### Phase 5: Structural Rough Cut

- Build the skeleton first: setup establishes the situation, development shows change and difficulty, landing gives result and feeling.
- Keep at least one of information or emotion; if neither survives, swap the footage or add narration.
- Output: structural cut. Checkpoint: three acts complete, and the reason to watch appears within the first 30 seconds.

### Phase 6: Fine Cut

- Control rhythm with music and time jumps; make leaps across time legible, never teleport without signals.
- Captions fill in context (names, places, amounts); color work for consistency and light styling.
- Output: master file. Checkpoint: old viewers are not bored, new viewers with zero context can follow.

### Phase 7: Publish & Review

- Titles and thumbnails follow [Packaging & Distribution](../../methods/packaging.md); lifestyle content favors personality-on-camera images.
- Fill in the four-layer data after 48 hours; lifestyle content watches follower conversion and return visits most.
- Output: publish pack and review notes. Checkpoint: note the personality thread or information gap to strengthen next time.

## 4. Workflow Variants by Type

### 4.1 Vlogs

- Difference from the standard workflow: weak event promise, high weight on personality; footage collection is ongoing rather than project-based.
- Adjusted flow: an always-on footage library → find the topic inside the footage → pick up closing shots → determine structure while editing → publish.

### 4.2 Travel Documentary

- Difference: the shooting window is one-shot, missed means lost; the itinerary itself is the shooting plan.
- Adjusted flow: advance research (information for guides, visual references for cinematic) → organize footage daily on the road → intensive edit after returning → publish (with information beats for guide-style).

### 4.3 Food & Shop Visits

- Difference: fast shooting, large volumes, and the conversion from process to finished result; shop visits often carry sponsored elements.
- Adjusted flow: menu or recipe research → fast on-site shooting (repeat key actions from several angles) → rough cut the same day → record narration → check disclosure and review boundaries before publishing.

### 4.4 Outdoor & Sports

- Difference: safety constraints outrank shooting needs; gear and shooting strategy are limited by the environment.
- Adjusted flow: route and risk plan → light gear plan → shoot key moments (leave the rest to action cams) → reconstruct the narrative back home → safety and permit check before publishing.

### 4.5 Event & Wedding Records

- Difference: a run-of-show drives the shooting and key moments never repeat; the delivery serves the host and the guests.
- Adjusted flow: align run-of-show and must-get list with the host → multi-camera capture → cut a broadcast version and a keepsake version per delivery goals → deliver and confirm rights.

## 5. Common Pitfalls

1. **Recording equals content.** A log without a goal or selection is not a work, no matter how much footage it has.
2. **Staging seen through.** Once arranged-ness outweighs authenticity, all trust resets to zero.
3. **Mood without a landing.** Information or emotion must deliver at least one clear payoff.
4. **Treating length as depth.** Length should serve the content, not the "effort" behind it.
5. **Blurred sponsored boundaries.** Handled badly, disclosure and honest review in shop visits and unboxings lose both the audience and the brand's trust.
6. **Leaning on viral moments.** Steady daily output is the base; a viral hit is an amplifier, not an engine.
7. **Skipping background.** Old viewers feel like acquaintances, new viewers are still strangers; every piece must work with zero context.
