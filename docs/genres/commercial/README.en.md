## 1. The Method & Applicable Types

The essence of commercial production: **translating a business need into acceptable deliverables**. The biggest difference from self-driven content is the evaluation system: the client's signature releases payment, platform rules decide whether it can go live, and audience data decides whether there is a next order. All three standards must hold for the piece to pass.

It suits organizations and brands with budget and clear goals; for individual creators, it is one path to converting skills directly into income.

### Applicable Types in Detail

| Type | Characteristics | Typical Length | Production Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ad films | One memory point and visual spectacle | 15–60 s | Concept first; take one memory point to its limit, simplify the rest |
| Brand films | Corporate narrative and human stories | 1–5 min | Carry abstract values with concrete people and events |
| Product demos | Features and selling points presented | 1–3 min | Structured selling points; usage scenarios visible |
| Launch & event videos | Process records and distribution | 30 s–10 min | Run-of-show drives shooting; livestream and quick-cut planned together |
| E-commerce videos | Conversion-oriented | 15 s–3 min | Trust elements (display, comparison, proof) plus compliant wording |
| Corporate training | Knowledge delivery and assessment | 3–30 min | Modular structure; verifiable learning objectives |
| Employer branding & recruiting | Talent attraction | 1–5 min | Real employees and real scenes |
| Government & enterprise films | Outcomes and history reported | 3–10 min | Steady structure; material and messaging reviewed in rounds |
| Trade-show recaps | Immediate distribution | 30 s–3 min | Same-day output; templated process |
| Music videos | Built around a music release | 2–5 min | Concept matches the song's character; cut multiple versions (full and vertical) |
| Motion graphics & brand idents | Motion-design-driven delivery | 15 s–2 min | Brand guidelines first; templatize for client reuse |
| Real estate & venue videos | Space presentation as the core | 30 s–3 min | Spatial narrative flow; wide angles plus stabilization |
| Restaurant & store promos | Store and product display | 15 s–2 min | Dishes and atmosphere in parallel; compliant wording |
| Investment & project pitches | Project value presentation | 3–10 min | Verify data and qualifications; steady structure |
| Concert & live music films | Professional records of live performances | 1–10 min | Balance multi-cam and live sound; artist approval |

### Boundaries & Scope

- The commercial nature does not change the method: brand-run content produced on a free creative workflow classifies under that method; projects needing client sign-off belong here.
- Commercial production often combines other methods (interview, animation, documentary); in combination, reuse each method page's execution notes for its part.
- The full method for budgets and contracting lives in [Monetization & Business](../../methods/monetization.md).

## 2. Key Principles

1. **Translating the need is step one.** Turn the client's "we want" into an acceptable goal: who watches, what they do afterwards, how it is measured. Get the translation wrong and everything after it is wrong.
2. **The concept must fit in one sentence.** Only what a non-professional reviewer grasps in ten seconds stands a chance of passing.
3. **Specs before creativity.** Length, aspect ratio, delivery versions and placement platforms are locked before the shoot; changing specs on set is the main cause of runaway cost.
4. **Three-fold acceptance.** The client is satisfied (sign-off), the platform is compliant (rules and advertising law), the audience responds (data) — none can be dropped.
5. **Process and version management.** Confirmation gates at script, storyboard, rough cut and final; revision counts written into the agreement to prevent endless rework.
6. **The attention rules still hold.** Commercial content must also grip viewers in the first seconds, tell one selling point and show evidence; "the client likes it" does not replace "the audience keeps watching."

## 3. Standard Workflow

Commercial production is driven by contract milestones: every phase ends in a confirmation, and no confirmation means no next phase. Process rigor decides the profit — not the creative quality.

| Phase | Key Actions | Output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Needs & Goals | Requirement translation, acceptance criteria, budget range | Needs confirmation |
| 2. Concept & Proposal | Concept, script, storyboard, quotation | Proposal and quotation |
| 3. Production Prep | Schedule, locations, crew, contract and insurance | Shooting plan and contract |
| 4. Shooting | On-set execution, monitoring, client confirmation points | Footage and on-site confirmation |
| 5. Post & Revision Rounds | Rough cut, feedback, fine cut (within agreed rounds) | Version chain |
| 6. Delivery & Settlement | Spec kits, rights and footage transfer, invoicing | Delivery pack and settlement sheet |
| 7. Review & Reuse | Case archive, renewal discussions | Case file and renewal plan |

### Phase 1: Needs & Goals

- Align three things with the client: who watches, what they do afterwards, how it is measured; turn vague statements into options for the client to choose.
- Clarify the budget range and the decision chain (who signs off, who accepts), into a needs confirmation sheet.
- Output: needs confirmation. Checkpoint: the acceptance criteria are quantifiable or demonstrable.

### Phase 2: Concept & Proposal

- State the concept in one sentence; script and storyboard to executable detail; the quotation lists inclusions and change-order rules.
- Offer two or three directions to choose from, avoiding the revision spiral of a single proposal.
- Output: proposal and quotation. Checkpoint: a non-professional reviewer can retell the core idea.

### Phase 3: Production Prep

- The schedule locks shooting days and key milestones; locations, crew, props and talent confirmed item by item.
- The contract states deliverables, revision counts, rights and payment milestones; high-risk shoots get insurance confirmed.
- Output: shooting plan and contract. Checkpoint: every dependency has an owner and a deadline.

### Phase 4: Shooting

- Execute per the plan; set client confirmation points on key shots to avoid major rework later.
- Back up footage the same day; the shooting log records usable takes and pickup lists.
- Output: footage and on-site confirmation. Checkpoint: check against the storyboard before wrap; fill gaps on the spot.

### Phase 5: Post & Revision Rounds

- The rough cut presents structure and narrative first, with feedback focused on structure; revisions stay within the agreed rounds.
- Changes beyond the agreed scope follow the change-order process, communicated early to avoid conflict.
- Output: version chain. Checkpoint: every feedback round has a written record and clear ownership.

### Phase 6: Delivery & Settlement

- Assemble spec kits per placement platform (horizontal and vertical, subtitled and clean); footage and rights transfer per contract.
- Invoicing and final payment follow the milestones; the client signs the delivery confirmation.
- Output: delivery pack and settlement sheet. Checkpoint: all deliverables checked off item by item.

### Phase 7: Review & Reuse

- Archive the project: final cut, storyboard, run sheets, budget, into a case file.
- Discuss renewal and series directions with the client; keep a follow-up cadence for unsigned leads.
- Output: case file and renewal plan. Checkpoint: the case file can go straight into the next proposal.

## 4. Workflow Variants by Type

### 4.1 Ad Films

- Difference from the standard workflow: the concept carries the most weight, confirmed through repeated rounds before production; budget concentrates in a few high-value shots.
- Adjusted flow: multiple concept rounds → storyboard precise to the shot → precision shooting (few setups, many repeats) → full attention to edit and sound in post.

### 4.2 Brand Films

- Difference: abstract values need people and stories to carry them; many subjects to interview makes scheduling complex.
- Adjusted flow: theme distillation (values in one sentence) → subject selection and pre-interviews → multi-location shooting → narration and music set the tone → multiple edited versions.

### 4.3 Launch & Event Videos

- Difference: the on-site schedule is uncontrollable, so shooting rehearses against the run-of-show; delivery deadlines are tight (often same-day).
- Adjusted flow: run-of-show alignment and camera plot → multi-camera capture → tiered delivery: a quick cut (same day) and a fine cut (within a week).

### 4.4 E-commerce Videos

- Difference: conversion goals are explicit and selling-point structure is high; compliant wording constrains heavily.
- Adjusted flow: selling-point mapping and script → product and scene shooting → multiple versions (detail page, placements, livestream clips) → compliance check → data collection.

### 4.5 Corporate Training

- Difference: knowledge accuracy and assessment goals come first, pictures stay simple; courseware and question banks often accompany it.
- Adjusted flow: learning objectives and module breakdown → script and courseware in parallel → recording or animation → review (business and compliance) → launch and learning-data collection.

## 5. Common Pitfalls

1. **Shooting on art-film instincts.** A concept the reviewer cannot grasp is a concept that does not exist.
2. **Rolling without acceptance criteria.** The client's internal opinions are not aligned, and all rework cost lands on you.
3. **Unlimited revisions.** Without a revision cap, profit hits zero after the third cut.
4. **Ignoring compliance.** Ad labels, qualifications and category-specific wording — the account and the brand take the damage.
5. **Delivery divorced from placement.** Specs, lengths and caption safe areas not designed for the actual placements.
6. **Serving only the client, not the audience.** If nobody finishes watching, the next order never comes.
