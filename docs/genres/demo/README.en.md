# Creator Atlas · Production Methods · Demo & Screencast

> Positioning: Separating "being able to do" from "learning by watching" — the demonstrator already has the skill; the viewer needs to learn it, or believe it. Credibility comes from a visible process.

## 1. The Method & Applicable Types

The essence of demo & screencast: **letting the process itself be the content**. Tutorials, reviews and gameplay share one thing — seeing is believing: conclusions come first as pictures, and persuasion comes from a checkable process rather than rhetoric.

It suits everything that must be seen to be believed or followed to be learned. Judge the viewer's level explicitly: the same operation needs every step stopped for a beginner, and only the key nodes for an experienced user.

### Applicable Types in Detail

| Type | Characteristics | Typical Length | Production Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Software & tool tutorials | Step-wise teaching with clear goals | 3–15 min | Step granularity follows the viewer's level; state environment and version |
| Gameplay, commentary & guides | Understanding gameplay, efficient information | 10–40 min (cuttable) | Editing compresses repeated operations; personality-driven commentary fills the gaps |
| Game guides & walkthroughs | Goal-directed, clear nodes | 5–20 min | Lead with the conclusion; slow down or annotate key nodes |
| Tech reviews | Controlled variables, stated conclusions | 5–15 min | Write down test methods and criteria; keep fact and opinion apart |
| Unboxing | First-contact experience | 3–10 min | Keep genuine reactions; no skipped steps |
| Teardowns & PC builds | Craft analysis and traceability | 8–30 min | Follow the teardown order; close-ups of parts and craft are the trust source |
| Crafts & DIY | Process and finished object | 5–15 min | Close-ups of key steps; list materials and tools |
| Repair & restoration | Before-and-after contrast | 5–20 min | The contrast is the value anchor; no skipping key steps |
| Cooking demonstrations | Step presentation meant to be followed | 3–10 min | Quantify amounts and heat; clear pauses between steps |
| Fitness & makeup follow-alongs | Movement demonstration and rhythm | 5–20 min | Fixed camera angles; safety notes and body-type boundaries |
| Experiments & tests | A verification process | 3–12 min | Controlled variables; reproducible conclusions |
| Workflow walkthroughs | The complete process on display | 5–15 min | The whole flow visible; speed up the information-free stretches |

### Boundaries & Scope

- Tutorials with heavy talking but a demo as the backbone belong here; talking-led content with supporting demos belongs to Talking to Camera.
- A review's commercial nature (review units, sponsorship) does not change the classification, but raises the bar for disclosure and criteria.
- Gameplay, commentary and guides classify here by their "screen capture plus commentary" production; the subject being games does not change the method.

## 2. Key Principles

1. **Result first.** Show the finished object or the conclusion before unfolding the process; the promise-and-payoff of this method takes the form of "see the effect first."
2. **Step granularity follows the viewer's level.** Make every "obvious to the author" explicit; beginner content would rather be slow, expert content would rather skip.
3. **Error prevention beats process narration.** Demonstrating the mistake viewers are most likely to make is the most underrated trust investment.
4. **Information clarity is a hard constraint.** Screen recordings live or die on font size and pointers; live-action demos on focus and light; what the viewer cannot see does not exist.
5. **Rhythm comes from speed division.** Speed up information-free stretches, run key actions at normal speed; a demo at one speed throughout will always drag.
6. **Reproducibility is the moat.** Tutorials and reviews must state their conditions: version, environment, parameters, criteria; a conclusion that cannot be reproduced has no value.
7. **Narration and operation use separate channels.** Talk less while operating and stop hands while talking; sound fighting action loses both information and watchability.

## 3. Standard Workflow

Demo production is built around **reproducibility**: design the step structure and the viewer's level first, then record, then push information density to its ceiling in the edit. Flow design before recording matters more than the technical quality of the recording.

| Phase | Key Actions | Output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Goal & Audience | Define the learning goal and the viewer's level; set step granularity | Audience profile and granularity setting |
| 2. Flow Design | Break into steps, plan error demos and verification | Step script and recording checklist |
| 3. Recording Prep | Clean environment, lock parameters, check fonts and focus | Environment and parameter snapshot |
| 4. Recording | Step-by-step capture, keep failures, separate narration, tag key points | Footage and chapter tags |
| 5. Speed & Structure | Divide speeds, build chapters, set information rhythm | Structural cut |
| 6. Reproducibility Check | Write down version, parameters, conditions; follow the video end to end | Reproducibility checklist |
| 7. Publish & Q&A | Pinned notes, comment errata, data review | Publish pack and errata log |

### Phase 1: Goal & Audience

- Write the learning goal: what the viewer can do afterwards. Then set the level: step-by-step for beginners, key nodes only for the experienced.
- Judge the level from real questions in the comments, not from your own comfort zone.
- Output: audience profile and granularity setting. Checkpoint: you can state "for whom, solving what."

### Phase 2: Flow Design

- Break the operation into a step list; each step states what to do, why, and the common mistake.
- Design verification: after every few steps, how does the viewer confirm they got it right?
- Output: step script and recording checklist. Checkpoint: no implicit prerequisites hide between steps.

### Phase 3: Recording Prep

- Screen work: larger resolution and fonts, notifications silenced, desktop cleared. Live action: focus locked, lighting constant.
- Fix every variable and take a screenshot of it (version number, parameters, environment) — it goes into the description later.
- Output: environment and parameter snapshot. Checkpoint: a 30-second test recording stays legible on a small screen.

### Phase 4: Recording

- Record in segments by step; keep failed attempts on camera (material for error demos); call out markers or tag key nodes.
- Separate narration from operation: perform a step silently first, then record the explanation separately, instead of doing and talking at once.
- Output: footage and chapter tags. Checkpoint: every step has both a "works" take and a "mistake" take.

### Phase 5: Speed & Structure

- Speed up the information-free stretches (4× and up); key actions run at normal speed, with text or progress cues over the fast parts.
- Organize chapters around the learning goal; move the conclusion and finished result to the opening.
- Output: structural cut. Checkpoint: watching only the speed-ups and chapter titles, the overall flow still makes sense.

### Phase 6: Reproducibility Check

- Follow the finished video end to end in a clean environment: do the same steps produce the same result?
- Write version, parameters, conditions and criteria into the description or on-screen notes; mark scope and exceptions clearly.
- Output: reproducibility checklist. Checkpoint: another person following the video gets the same result.

### Phase 7: Publish & Q&A

- Pin a comment with errata and update notes; collect frequent comment questions as next episode topics.
- Fill in the data after 48 hours; tutorials watch saves and return visits, reviews watch comment quality.
- Output: publish pack and errata log. Checkpoint: every known erratum is in the pinned comment or the description.

## 4. Workflow Variants by Type

### 4.1 Software & Tool Tutorials

- Difference from the standard workflow: versions iterate fast and can expire within half a year; environments vary widely, raising the reproducibility bar.
- Adjusted flow: lock version and platform → step script → screen recording (segmented) → speed and captions → reproducibility check → publish with the applicable version stated.

### 4.2 Gameplay & Commentary

- Difference: the footage is the play session itself — high volume and repetitive; commentary's information efficiency sets the final density.
- Adjusted flow: long play recording → tag highlights and key nodes → cut compresses repeated operations → record commentary → publish (as a series or one-off).

### 4.3 Reviews & Comparisons

- Difference: test design precedes shooting, and controlled variables are the core asset; conclusions separate fact from opinion.
- Adjusted flow: design the test plan → record the process (data, picture, environment) → organize conclusions → shoot the presentation → criteria check → publish.

### 4.4 Teardowns & Builds

- Difference: the process is irreversible (once apart, it must go back together), so shooting and operation must be planned together.
- Adjusted flow: plan the teardown path → dual recording (fixed camera plus close-up) → operate in order with logging → edit in teardown order → add craft close-ups.

### 4.5 Follow-along Classes (fitness, makeup, etc.)

- Difference: rhythm and angles are the core, viewers follow rather than understand, and safety and boundary notes must come first.
- Adjusted flow: choreograph the moves or flow → fixed-camera test shoot (rhythm check) → record for real (cues and beat) → add on-screen prompts and safety notes → publish.

## 5. Common Pitfalls

1. **Skipping steps.** The steps authors find obvious are exactly where beginners get stuck.
2. **Settling for poor recording quality.** Blur, clipping audio and a swinging cursor destroy watchability outright.
3. **Explaining what to do but never why.** Viewers learn the operation but not the judgment.
4. **Conclusions without criteria.** "Smoother" and "better" carry no test conditions — neither credible nor reproducible.
5. **Running in real time throughout.** Unwilling to speed up or cut, the information density bottoms out.
6. **Turning the demo into a read-aloud.** The rhythm of a demo comes from the operation itself; narration follows the process.
