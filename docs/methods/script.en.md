## 1. What This Stage Solves

A script is the design of promise and payoff: **the opening makes a promise (the hook), the middle delivers it, the close returns value (emotion or new knowledge)**. Lose any one of the three and viewers feel either "the title lied" or "I finished it and still cannot say what I watched."

Four problems this stage solves:

| Symptom | Root Cause | Section |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Swiped away in the first 3 s | No promise and no information gap in the opening | §2.1, §2.2 |
| Completion rate drops mid-video | Flat pacing, uneven information density | §2.4 |
| Nothing sticks after watching | Loose structure; the close neither wraps up nor returns value | §2.3, §2.6 |
| Missing footage discovered after the shoot | Shooting started before the script was locked | §3, §4 |

The cost of two common approaches:

- **No script, thinking on the fly while shooting**: footage ends up scattered and reshoots multiply; structural problems surface only at the editing table, where rework costs the most; runtime and information load both run out of control.
- **Outline only, wording left to the moment**: the structure exists, but wording and rhythm are undecided; improvised delivery drifts into written-style phrasing and logic jumps, and three promised points arrive as two.

Scope of this page: how to write talking-head scripts, narrative scripts, tutorial scripts and interview paper-edit scripts, plus the delivery format from script to storyboard. Camera language, lighting and audio belong to Production & Shooting; final pacing and sound design belong to Editing & Post-Production; titles and thumbnails belong to Packaging & Distribution.

Standard output: **a locked script plus a shoot-ready storyboard deliverable**. Until the topic passes the packaging test, go back to [Positioning & Topic Selection](positioning.md) (§2, §3).

## 2. Core Framework

### 2.1 Opening Structure: The First 3 s and the First 30–45 s

The opening is designed in two segments, each with a hard job:

- **The first 3 s decide whether the viewer stays.** Do exactly one thing: create "this concerns me" or "something is off, I want to know why."
- **The first 30–45 s deliver the promise**, making three things clear: what this video gives, why it is worth staying for, and roughly how it unfolds (length and format).

Opening formula: **result up front / conflict / counterintuitive + information gap**.

| Element | Approach | Generic Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Result up front | Show the destination directly: the finished product, the conclusion or the image | "This is what day 90 looks like; on day 1 I could not have imagined it." |
| Conflict | Put the contradiction on the table (resources, time, stance) | "The budget covers one coffee; the ask is commercial-grade footage." |
| Counterintuitive | State a judgment that runs against intuition | "The more gear you own, the more wasted footage you shoot." |
| Information gap | Leave a question that only watching on can answer | "Why is the dumbest method actually the fastest?" |

Operating rule: if a single sentence cannot fit all 4 elements, do not force it; 2 is the minimum; a promise made in the opening must be explicitly paid off in the middle.

### 2.2 Hook Type Table

A hook is the concrete wording of the opening. Nine generic types; use 1 or 2 per video; stacking more reads as trying too hard.

| Hook Type | Mechanism | Generic Line Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Curiosity gap | Drop an unfinished story; leave a "what happened next" | "I thought three days would do it; on day 41 I was still revising." |
| Pain point | Name the trouble the viewer already has | "If you too only spot the blurry export at the end, this saves you two hours." |
| Benefit | State the payoff straight | "This workflow takes 5 hours of repetitive editing out of your week." |
| Conflict | Pose an opposition or a dilemma | "The client wants premium; the budget covers one coffee; I took a third path." |
| Numbers | Use specific counts to build credibility and curiosity | "17 shots tell the whole story; shot 9 is the turn." |
| Scene | Drop straight into a concrete situation | "2 a.m., sixth failed export; I decided to replace the entire workflow." |
| Counterintuitive | Overturn the default expectation | "Poor image quality usually has nothing to do with your gear." |
| Self-identification | Name a group so people slot themselves in | "Students just starting out can copy this setup as is." |
| Story opening | Start from a small story or detail | "On my first on-site shoot, I knocked over the client's light." |

Selection rule: knowledge content favors counterintuitive, numbers and benefit; documentary favors curiosity gap, story and scene; tutorial content favors pain point, benefit and scene. The hook must say the same thing as the title and thumbnail; for the thumbnail side see Packaging & Distribution.

### 2.3 Body Structure Templates

| Structure | Use Case | Suggested Time Split |
| --- | --- | --- |
| List | Experience roundups, pitfall guides, resource collections | Opening 15–20%, items share 65–70%, close 15%; items ordered by importance, descending |
| Q&A | Answer sessions, diagnosis, corrections | Question block 10%, answers 80%, recap 10%; each question maps to one information point |
| Suspense–development–payoff | Documentary, challenges, experiments, investigations | Suspense 15%, development 65%, payoff 20%; the payoff must answer the opening question |
| Compressed three-act | Story-driven content of 60–90 s | Setup 25%, escalation 50%, resolution 25% |
| Comparison | Reviews, plan comparisons, method pros and cons | Two threads alternating, switching sides every 60–90 s; a joint verdict at the close |
| Tutorial step-by-step | Hands-on teaching, process walkthroughs | Keep the total under 7 steps; each step covers "what to do, why, common pitfalls" |

How to choose: start from the video's payoff type. New knowledge points to list or Q&A; emotional experience to suspense–development–payoff or compressed three-act; decision support to comparison; hands-on learning to tutorial step-by-step.

### 2.4 Information Density & Pacing

Three hard rules:

- **A change every 3–5 s**: a new fact, a new example, a turn, a pause or a picture change all count.
- **White space**: give 0.5–1 s of digestion time after each information point; past 30 s of continuous delivery, insert an example or a pause.
- **Key sentence first**: put the conclusion at the top of the paragraph, then unfold the reasoning; viewers understand most easily in a conclusion-first order.

| Check | Standard | Warning Sign |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Information-point spacing | At least 1 retellable information point every 30 s | Stretches over 60 s that are all setup |
| Segment length | Long videos: no segment over 90 s; vertical short videos: no segment over 15 s | Continuous monologue, no examples or picture support |
| Change frequency | Once every 3–5 s | One screen throughout, zero pauses, zero turns |

### 2.5 Story Elements

| Element | Role | Cost If Missing | Action |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Stakes | Make viewers care about the outcome | Nothing is at stake; they can swipe away any time | Spell out "what failure would cost" |
| Conflict | Provide forward drive | Flat, no rise or fall | At least one obstacle per stage: resources, time, ability, stance |
| Concrete detail | Build credibility | Reads as invented or vague | Replace adjectives with numbers, names, actions |
| Reversal | Create a memory point | Nothing to say when it ends | Let the ending diverge from expectation; leave at least one "did not expect that" |

In documentary content, stakes have a natural source: **exposed vulnerability and real data**. Admitting mistakes, showing the real failure rate, publishing progress numbers not yet on target: these work better than dramatizing hardship. Boundary: expose only what you are willing to keep public long term; anonymize anything involving other people or commercial content first.

### 2.6 Closing Design

| Closing Move | Approach | Suggested Length |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wrap-up | Answer the question the opening raised, in one sentence | 5–15 s |
| Call to action | Give one concrete action and say why | One sentence, within 10 s |
| Next-episode hook | Tease what the next episode specifically covers; do not just say "see you next time" | One sentence |

Rule: 1 or 2 closing moves are enough; all three together dilute one another. The next-episode hook must land on concrete content, matching the next entry in the topic bank.

## 3. Workflow & Steps

Eight fixed steps in fixed order; every step has an output, and an unsettled output blocks the next step.

| Step | What to Do | Output | Pass Criteria |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 Topic confirmation | Pull one topic that has passed the packaging test from the topic bank | Topic entry | One sentence covers who it is for and why now |
| 2 One-line core | Write the core promise of this video | One-line core | Read it aloud once: would you stop for it yourself? |
| 3 Write the close and opening first | Fix the payoff and the promise before the middle | Opening and closing draft | The close answers the opening |
| 4 Body outline | Pick a structure template per §2.3 and fill in the points | Outline of 5–9 points | Every point maps to one information point |
| 5 Section-by-section draft | Write it out section by section as spoken copy | Complete first draft | All spoken short sentences, no blocks of written style |
| 6 Read-aloud test | Read it out loud, time it, mark the stumbles | Marked draft | Within 10% of the target length |
| 7 Cut about 20% | Trim redundancy per §2.4; cut 15–25% from the first draft | Locked script | The promise still stands complete after the cuts |
| 8 Deliver the storyboard | Convert per §4 into a two-column AV script or storyboard table | Shoot-ready deliverable | The shooter can start from the deliverable alone |

Notes:

- Step 2 converges through one sentence pattern: **explain [one thing] to [an audience] so they [change how they think or act]**.
- Step 6 must be out loud; silent reading cannot expose tongue-twisters; the stumbles you mark are the top candidates for cuts in step 7.
- Step 7 cutting priority: repeated explanations, courtesy transitions, self-praise and disclaimers, side threads unrelated to the promise.

## 4. Templates & Tools

### 4.1 Three Script Formats

| Format | Use Case | Core Fields |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Talking-head script | Single presenter on camera; opinion and spoken delivery | Word-for-word copy, pause and stress marks, length estimate, backup lines for slips |
| Two-column AV script | Finished videos where picture and narration weigh equally | Left column: shot, shot size, footage number; right column: lines, sound effects, music cues |
| Outline plus paper edit | Content that cannot be pre-written: interviews, documentary, vlogs | Question list or shooting points, footage transcript, paper-edit numbers and running order |

Template entry points: [talking-head script template](../../templates/script-talking-head.md), [narrative script template](../../templates/script-narrative.md), [storyboard template](../../templates/storyboard.md). For writing and collaboration tools, see [Resources & Tools](../../resources/README.md); this page does not list them again.

### 4.2 Talking-Head Script Writing Rules

| Rule | Practice | Counter-Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Short sentences | Keep a sentence under 25 characters; one sentence, one meaning | Long sentences stacking two or more clauses |
| Colloquial words | Use connectors like "so", "actually", "you will find" | Written style like "based on the above discussion" |
| Pauses and stress | Mark breath points with slashes or line breaks; bold the stressed words | No marks anywhere; improvising on the spot |
| Speakable numbers | Turn big numbers into terms a person can feel | Reading "penetration up 37.2%" straight |

One overarching rule: the script is written for the human ear; read it out loud once before locking it (§3, step 6); wherever it reads awkwardly out loud, the writing had a problem too.

## 5. Data & Acceptance Criteria

### 5.1 Three Acceptance Checks

| Check | Standard | Pass Line | If It Fails |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Read-aloud length | Estimate 250–300 characters per minute (the usual range for spoken Chinese) | Within 10% of the target length | Cut whole sections; never rely on speeding up delivery |
| Information-point density | At least 1 retellable information point every 30 s | Spot-check 3 windows of 30 s; all pass | Add examples or cut setup |
| Friend test | Have one target viewer watch it, then ask them to retell the core point | The retell matches the one-line core | Back to §3 step 3; rewrite the promise and the close |

### 5.2 Opening 3-Second Self-Check

Run through every item before locking; fail any single one and rewrite the opening:

- [ ] Does the first sentence carry a promise, a conflict or a counterintuitive turn?
- [ ] Does it name a group, or trigger self-identification?
- [ ] Does the first piece of information match the title and thumbnail?
- [ ] Read it aloud: do the first 3 s contain filler ("hello everyone", "today", "just chatting")?
- [ ] By the end of 3 s, do viewers know "what they get by watching on" or "there is a question left open"?

## 6. Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Typical Symptom | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Opening with self-introduction | No promise given in the first 10 s | Move the self-introduction after the promise, or squeeze it to the close |
| Overlong setup | The first 30 s are all background | Break the background up; insert it into the sections that need it |
| Fake suspense | The promised answer never arrives, or has nothing to do with the promise | Write the close before the opening; if the answer cannot be written, change the promise |
| Information overload | 5 concepts stuffed into one minute, each skimmed | Keep only what serves the promise; save the rest for the next video |
| Written-style language | Constructions that trip the tongue on the read-aloud | Rewrite in spoken language; add the read-aloud test |
| No stakes | Viewers cannot tell what failure would cost | Add the cost of losing, or real data |
| Dragging close | 30 s of chitchat after the wrap-up | Pick one or two of wrap-up, action, hook; stop when done |

## 7. Where to Go Deeper

- **Series structure**: Upgrade single-video structure to a series structure: a fixed opening ritual, a fixed segment order, connecting hooks between episodes. Once serialized, audience expectations stabilize and topic pressure drops.
- **Talking head vs. picture ratio**: Three ratios for reference: talking-head-led (teaching, opinion), talking head alternating with B-roll (experiences, reviews), all B-roll with voice-over (documentary). Fix the ratio at the script stage; the shot list follows from it.
- **Paper-edit scripts for interview footage**: Cut the transcript into blocks by theme, number them, then reorder them into a "paper edit" along the narrative order. A paper edit needs no software, so restructuring costs close to nothing; enter the editing software only after it is locked.
- **Opening A/B experiments**: Prepare two openings for the same piece; after publishing, compare retention at 3 s and 10 s. Use enough samples, change one variable at a time, and record the conclusions back into the topic bank.
