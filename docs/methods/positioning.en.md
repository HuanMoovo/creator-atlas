# Creator Atlas · Method Domains · Positioning & Topic Selection

> Positioning: This page answers what video to make, for whom, and why it gets seen. It sits first in the creation chain, because it sets the ceiling for every stage after it.
> Companions: Script & Storytelling, Packaging & Distribution, Growth & Analytics, and the `docs/genres/` pages (topic conventions by type).
> Note: Statements about platform mechanics follow public sources and official documentation (verified 2026-10); confirm against the latest official wording before acting.

## 1. What This Stage Solves

One claim that holds: **the idea sets a video's ceiling; execution decides whether you reach it.** Shooting and editing, however polished, cannot rescue a topic nobody wants to click.

Four concrete problems this stage solves:

1. **No direction**: no idea what to shoot or for whom; trend-chasing buys homogenization and zero accumulation.
2. **A direction but no topics**: the direction is right, but subjects get grabbed on the fly before every shoot, so quality and cadence stay unstable.
3. **Topics but no viewers**: the content is fine, it just cannot get clicks; usually the topic and its "packagability" have come apart.
4. **Follower growth out of step with views**: views arrive but followers do not, or only returning viewers watch.

The standard output of this stage is two assets: a **one-line positioning** (for whom, what value, on what grounds) and a **topic bank** (30–50 candidate topics kept current, each with a title and a thumbnail idea).

## 2. Core Framework

### 2.1 The Three-Layer Audience Model: Core, Occasional, New

Split the people likely to see this video into three layers (a split commonly used in public write-ups):

| Layer | Who They Are | What It Means for Topics |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Core viewers | Watch every episode and know you | Guarantee the baseline; serving only them locks the view ceiling |
| Occasional viewers | Know you; decide by topic whether to watch | The growth engine; topics must use contexts they understand |
| New viewers | Meet you first through the recommendation feed or search | Decide breakout; need topics that hold with zero background knowledge |

Rule of thumb: run every topic through the "three-layer questions": Will core viewers want to watch it? Can occasional viewers follow it? How much prior knowledge do new viewers need? A topic that clears all three is ideal; one that clears only core should expect views to stall at the follower count.

### 2.2 The Three Conditions of a Good Topic

| Condition | Explanation | Self-check Question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Information gain or emotional value | New information, a new angle, or strong emotion (resonance, catharsis, curiosity) | Can viewers say "what I learned / what I felt" afterward? |
| Packagable | One title and one thumbnail can make the curiosity clear | Can you write 3 distinct titles right now? |
| Executable | Doable with current resources, and you are willing to do it | Are the shooting cost, time and risk acceptable? |

A topic missing any one of the three conditions goes back into the bank for rework.

### 2.3 The Topic Funnel: 100 to 1

How mature channels do it: **pick 1 of 100 ideas to turn into a video.** Mix ideas from three sources:

- **Internal sources**: experiences, data and methods only you have. The hardest pool to copy.
- **External sources**: comment questions, community threads, industry news, search terms. The demand is already proven; what is missing is your telling.
- **Packaging-driven**: start from a good title or thumbnail, then work backward to the content. It sounds utilitarian, but it filters out many topics that are fun to tell and boring to hear.

### 2.4 Benchmarking: Validate Demand, Not Copy Content

The object of benchmarking is **demand**, not **content**. Three steps:

1. **Find benchmarks**: 5–10 accounts in the same direction; look at the last 90 days, not historical hits.
2. **Break down hits**: pick 3 high-view videos from each account and record the topic angle, title approach, thumbnail elements, structure and length, engagement data. Put it in a table.
3. **Find gaps**: among proven, high-demand subjects, which ones still lack **your angle**?

One test: benchmarking should give you "what is proven to work," not "a template you have to follow." Copying content only yields homogenization.

## 3. Workflow & Steps

Positioning is a quarterly action; topic selection is a weekly one.

**A. Positioning (every quarter, or re-checked after every 30 videos)**

1. Write the one-line positioning: **for [audience], provide [value]; unlike [alternative], because [your difference]**.
2. Build the audience profile: who they currently watch, what they lack, and in what situations they watch your videos.
3. Draw content boundaries: state clearly what you will not do. A "not-doing list" prevents drift better than a to-do list.
4. Set the release cadence: pick a frequency you can sustain long-term; running low beats stopping.

**B. Topic Selection (one fixed session per week, 30–60 min)**

1. Restock: pull 5–10 new ideas from each of the three sources into the bank.
2. Evaluate: rough-screen against the three conditions in §2.2; delete what fails.
3. Package: for each passing topic, write 3 titles and 1 thumbnail sketch on the spot; failing that, the packagability bar is not met.
4. Order: sort by information gain × timeliness × execution cost, and pick next week's shoots.
5. Record: topics, titles, thumbnails and data all go into the bank; every published video must trace back to its original hypothesis.

## 4. Templates & Tools

- [Topic bank template](../../templates/topic-bank.md): fields include topic, source, target audience layer, title candidates, thumbnail idea, hypothesis, status, publish data.
- Topic scorecard: information gain / packagability / audience overlap / execution cost, 1–5 each; a total ≥14 approves the topic.
- Benchmark table: account, 90-day median views, Top3 subjects, title pattern, thumbnail pattern, points worth borrowing.
- Tool entries are in [Resources & Tools](../../resources/README.md): platform search and creator dashboards are the cheapest data sources; add third-party tools as needed.

## 5. Data & Acceptance Criteria

- Topic bank holds ≥30 items; net gain ≥5 per week.
- Candidate topics can turn into a "title + thumbnail sketch" during discussion; the share that cannot should stay under 20%.
- Review after publishing: record predicted performance alongside actuals; check the hit rate a month later. Hit rate is the only scoreboard for this skill.
- When data is off, check in this order: topic and packaging first (impressions and clicks), then the first 30 s (retention), and only then suspect execution details (see Growth & Analytics §2).

## 6. Common Mistakes

1. **Treating trend-chasing as strategy**: trends are ammunition, not a direction; ride one occasionally, but never build a channel on them.
2. **Self-indulgent topics**: the session runs on what you find cool, and nobody asks why viewers would watch.
3. **Jumping fields too often**: recommender systems and viewers both build expectations on stability; a channel that switches direction three times a month keeps neither side. Keep the field-versus-experiment split at around 80/20.
4. **Serving only core viewers**: views stall at the follower count; chase only new traffic instead, and core viewers drift away.
5. **Shooting without a packaging test**: if you cannot write the title and thumbnail, the topic itself is usually still vague.
6. **Copying benchmark content instead of breaking down demand**: you get homogenization, not differentiation.
7. **Not maintaining the topic bank**: updating only when you remember equals having no bank.
8. **No "not-doing list"**: touching everything leaves nothing.
9. **Making "easy to shoot" the first criterion**: the topic that is least trouble to produce is often the lowest bar to entry.

## 7. Where to Go Deeper

- **Institutionalize the topic session**: put the weekly session on the calendar; in multi-person teams, fix roles (idea pitching, challenging, packaging tests kept apart).
- **Feed data back into intuition**: calibrate "what I think viewers want" against your channel's click and retention data; count the topic hit rate monthly.
- **Series and product lines**: topics with stable performance upgrade into a series (fixed structure, fixed audience expectations); series then grow into courses or paid columns.
- **Cross-platform topic adaptation**: the same topic has different expression limits in long-form video and vertical short-form content; adaptation specs are in Packaging & Distribution §5.
