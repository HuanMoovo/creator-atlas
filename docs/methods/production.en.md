## 1. What This Stage Solves

Shooting translates a script into usable footage. It has a brutal property: **how complete this stage is sets the ceiling for the edit.** Editing can rescue pace and structure; it cannot rescue what was never shot, never in focus, or never recorded. One standard is enough when making the call on set: can the editor use this clip as it stands?

**Audio matters more than image quality.** That ranking comes from real audience experience, for three reasons:

- A slightly worse picture is forgiven: phone screens are small, viewing happens in casual settings, mild noise and soft focus are usually tolerated, and platform compression flattens image-quality differences even further.
- Slightly worse audio drives viewers away: noise, echo and jumpy volume keep draining attention, and that drain cannot be paid back by toughing it out; it often pushes people off within the first tens of seconds.
- In talking-to-camera video the information lives in the sound: opinions, data and emotion all ride on the spoken voice, and picture quality is only the backdrop.

Four specific problems this stage solves:

1. **Incomplete coverage**: wide and medium shots are done per the storyboard, medium close-ups are missed, and the edit has nothing to cut to.
2. **Technically unusable**: soft focus, overexposure, underexposure, clipped audio; discovered only after the shoot, when a retake costs double.
3. **Sound below standard**: ambient noise, room echo, plosive pops, unstable volume; noise reduction in the edit cannot rescue it.
4. **Stiff delivery**: reading to the lens as if from a page, flat pace, no stress, no pauses; viewers swipe away within seconds.

Standard output: a **footage package ready for the edit**, containing compliant picture, backup shots free of continuity breaks, a room-tone sample of the recording environment, and files named under one consistent rule.

## 2. Core Framework

### 2.1 Visual Language: Shot Size, Composition, Camera Position

Shot size is the punctuation of storytelling. The general rhythm for a talking-to-camera or narrative video: medium shots carry most of the information, medium close-ups stress key sentences, close-ups serve emotion and detail, and wide and extreme wide shots mostly appear in the opening and transitions.

| Shot size | Frame range | Main use | Pacing reference |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Extreme wide | The person's relationship to the environment | Opening orientation, establishing time and place | 1–2 uses per video, each no longer than 3 s |
| Wide | Full body plus part of the environment | Action display, body language | Use with action-heavy passages |
| Medium | Waist up | The default shot size for talking to camera and dialogue | The bulk of the footage |
| Medium close-up | Chest up | Stress points, closing the distance | Cut to it on key arguments |
| Close-up | Face, hands, objects | Emotion, detail, insert shots | No longer than 2–3 s at a time |

Three composition rules to remember:

- Rule of thirds: keep the eyes near the top third line of the frame; keep background horizontals (horizon, counter line) from cutting across the neck.
- Looking room: when the subject faces one side, leave more space in the direction of the gaze than behind the head; reversed, the frame feels cramped.
- Headroom: leave about one fist of space above the head; too much looks empty, too little looks tight.

Camera height: level with the eyes or slightly above. High angles shrink presence, low angles enlarge nostrils; use these two extremes only when they clearly help the content. When shooting on a phone, raise it to eye level; do not prop it on a desk.

### 2.2 Lighting: Squeeze the Available Light First

Lighting is a matter of priority, not quantity: **natural light > one soft light > multi-light setups**.

- **Window light**: move the shooting position to about 45 degrees to the front side of a window so the light falls across the face at an angle; keep the subject 1–2 m from the window, since too close gives harsh contrast and too far loses the effect. Window light on an overcast day is a free softbox.
- **Golden hour**: about 1 hour after sunrise and before sunset, with warm color temperature and a low light position; the most stable window for faces on location.
- **Fill**: in a window-light scene, put a reflector or white foam board on the shadow side; within 1 m of the subject the effect shows.

Move to three-point lighting only when shooting from fixed positions over the long run:

| Light | Position | Purpose | Note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Key light | About 45 degrees to the front side of the subject, slightly above eye height | Sets the face's light and shade and its depth | Use a large diffuser or softbox where possible |
| Fill light | Opposite the key light, at about 1/2 to 1/3 of its power | Lifts dark shadows, controls contrast | A reflector can take its place |
| Rim light | Behind and off to the side of the subject, aimed at the hair and shoulder line | Separates the subject from the background | Power can stay low; one small light is enough |

Three high-frequency failure scenes:

- **Overhead light**: light coming straight down leaves eye sockets and the area under the nose full of shadow; drop the light to slightly above eye height.
- **Split face**: one half bright, one half dark, with contrast too strong; add fill light or a reflector, or turn the subject 15–30 degrees.
- **Mixed color temperatures**: window light plus a warm ceiling lamp leaves one side of the face yellow and the other blue; before rolling, switch off light sources with stray color temperatures and bring every light to one color temperature.

### 2.3 Sound: Solve Capture First, Everything Else Later

Audio capture setups, ranked by real-world results:

| Priority | Option | Where it fits | Note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Lavalier microphone | Talking to camera, interviews, moving shots | Closest to the mouth; the smallest share of ambient noise |
| 2 | Shotgun mic (directional) | Fixed positions, multi-camera | Strong directionality, but from a distance it collects room echo |
| 3 | On-camera mic | A stopgap for moving shots | A little better than built-in; still, in essence, a mic at a distance |
| 4 | Phone or camera built-in mic | Emergency use | Distance decides everything; swap it out whenever you can |

Recording essentials, four on-set disciplines:

- **Levels**: keep peaks between -12dB and -6dB. Hitting near 0dB is clipping and cannot be saved; recording too low lets room tone take too high a share, and that is hard to save too.
- **Monitoring**: wear monitoring headphones the whole session. Level meters on a camera screen lie; ears do not.
- **Room-tone sample**: before the real take, record 30 s of room tone (nobody moving, nobody speaking), to serve as the noise reference for noise reduction in the edit.
- **Room treatment (low cost)**: turn off air conditioning and fans; add soft furnishings to the frame (curtains, fabric sofa, rug, bookshelf); do not sit against a wall, keep 1 m or more behind you; in a hard-walled room with obvious echo, switch to a lavalier first.

### 2.4 On-Camera Delivery: Make Viewers Forget You Are Reading

- **Using a teleprompter**: rewrite the script into spoken language before it goes in (written sentences always come out awkward aloud); use large type, wide line spacing, 2–3 lines per screen; scroll slightly slower than you speak. Do not chase the words with your eyes: hold one sentence, say it to the lens, then glance at the next.
- **Do not let viewers catch you reading**: even pace, no stress, a fixed stare are the giveaways. The fix is to mark key sentences and slow down with a pause when you reach them; allow natural slips and breaths between sections.
- **Colloquial wording**: read the finished script straight through; shorten any sentence that trips the tongue. Swap written connectors (therefore, however, moreover) for spoken ones (so, but, and).
- **Energy level**: the lens eats about 20% of your expressive energy. Roll one notch above your everyday speaking state; on playback, listening for whether the ending runs out of steam is a simple self-check.
- **Lens or listener**: look into the lens when addressing viewers; when explaining an object or talking with someone, let the gaze drift away naturally. Do not let the eyes jump back and forth within one sentence.
- **Retake discipline**: at most three takes per shot. If three takes fail, the problem is usually the script or the preparation; stop and revise the script. Pushing on only produces stiffer versions.

## 3. Workflow & Steps

Run the shoot day in this order:

**A. Scouting and rehearsal (1–3 days before the shoot)**

1. Walk the location and check three things: where the light comes from, where the noise comes from, where the power is.
2. Test-shoot 30 s on a phone: check composition, and listen to the ambient sound on playback.
3. Mark camera positions and blocking points (tape on the floor) and estimate how long each shot will take.

**B. Gear check (15 minutes before rolling; three fixed items)**

| Check | What it covers | Pass standard |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Batteries | Camera/phone, microphone, lights, spare batteries | Battery level ≥80% on every device; spares fully charged |
| Storage | Free capacity and card count | Free space ≥ 2x the estimated footage volume |
| Audio | Mic connections, meters moving, test-recording playback | 30 s test recording; playback clear with no noise |

**C. Shoot to the storyboard**

- Hardest first: shoot the most physically taxing, most light-dependent, most error-prone shots early; leave the easy talking-to-camera pieces for later.
- Main shots before support shots: finish each segment's main shot first, then add insert and backup coverage in one pass, to cut down on changing positions.
- On every change of camera position or shot size, spot-check 10–20 s of playback, listening for sound and watching for focus.

**D. On-set playback spot checks**: play back every important clip on the spot; check focus, exposure and audio levels. If it fails, reshoot immediately; do not count on the edit to save it.

**E. Double backup of footage**: complete two copies on the day the shoot ends (card → computer → portable drive or cloud). Do not format the card until both backups have passed verification (playable, matching file sizes).

**F. Naming and handoff**: name files as `date_project_scene_shot-number_shot-size_version`, for example `20261007_ChannelA_S2_012_MediumShot_v2`. Attach shoot notes to the footage: which takes are usable, which need reshoots, and where the room-tone file sits.

## 4. Templates & Tools

The ordering principle for gear upgrades: **sound first, then lighting, camera last**. Viewers are more sensitive to sound than to picture, and the marginal returns of the three roughly follow that order.

| Tier | Typical setup | Stage it fits | Budget scale (CNY, rough) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Entry | Phone + lavalier mic + tripod | Videos 1 to 50 | Hundreds of CNY |
| Intermediate | Phone or camera + shotgun or dual lavaliers + one soft light + reflector | Steady publishing, taking on sponsored work | Thousands of CNY |
| Pro | Camera + multi-mic setup + three-point lighting + monitor | Teams, multi-camera, location work | Tens of thousands of CNY and up |

- For specific models and buying entry points, see [Shooting Gear](../../resources/gear.md).
- Storyboard sheets and shooting checklists live in [Checklists & Templates](../../templates/README.md).
- Before shooting, build a "shoot-day checklist" in your phone notes: the three gear checks, storyboard order, room-tone recording, double backup, ticked off item by item against the §3 workflow.

## 5. Data & Acceptance Criteria

**Usable-footage rate**: the passing line is 60%–80% of one shoot's footage going straight into the edit. Below 60%, go back over the preparation and checking steps in §3; the usual cause is a skipped test shoot or spot check.

Technical checklist to run through for every clip:

| Check | Pass standard | If it fails |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Focus | Eyes or subject edges sharp | Reshoot on the spot |
| Exposure | No blown whites on the face; shadow detail retained | Adjust, then reshoot |
| Audio levels | Peaks -12dB to -6dB, no clipping | Adjust gain and re-record |
| White balance | Skin tones natural and consistent through a scene | Unify color temperature, then reshoot |
| Frame rate & shutter | Frame rate matches the project; shutter at double the frame rate (1/50 for 25p) | Set and lock before rolling |

A clip counts as "usable" only when all four hold at once:

1. Focus is solid, exposure sits inside the range, sound is clearly audible.
2. 2–3 s of head and tail room, so the edit can find cut points.
3. The content matches the storyboard's intent; takes that get a key line wrong do not go into "usable".
4. The file is intact: it plays through to the end with no corrupt frames.

## 6. Common Mistakes

1. **Rolling before checking**: going straight into the real take without a test recording or test shoot; problems surface on the editing table.
2. **Upgrading picture, never sound**: a new camera, but audio still from the built-in mic; the finished feel drops instead of rising.
3. **A single shot size**: one medium shot carried from top to bottom, leaving the edit nothing to work with.
4. **No room-tone sample**: noise reduction has no reference, and the sound ends up muffled.
5. **Eyes on the teleprompter throughout**: a flat stare that shows viewers the reading at a glance.
6. **Retaking to numbness**: ten takes of the same shot, each worse than the last; the three-take cap never enforced.
7. **No on-set playback**: skipping spot checks, finding soft focus at home, and having to reschedule the shoot.
8. **Formatting after one backup**: wiping the card on the spot; a data accident nothing can undo.
9. **Careless naming**: `IMG_0421` files everywhere, and no way to find a shot during the edit.
10. **Ignoring room acoustics**: recording talking-to-camera audio in a hard-walled room with obvious echo; no mic can rescue it.

## 7. Where to Go Deeper

- **Multi-camera**: two or more cameras covering different shot sizes, switched freely in the edit. The key is matching frame rate and white balance, and aligning sound and picture before rolling with one sync action (a clap).
- **Building a B-roll library**: on every shoot, record 5–10 extra shots of environment, detail and action, filed by theme; once the library builds up, the edit no longer needs dedicated pickups.
- **Batch shoot days**: record several videos from one setup, spreading the switching cost of lights, positions and wardrobe; suits talking-to-camera and explainer channels.
- **Location contingencies**: check the weather and backup locations before setting out; carry a reflector, a wind muff and a spare mic. When the light turns, protect the sound first and fall back to a usable shot size.
